# Calibrating a voice

Build a working view of how the author notices, thinks, and addresses the reader. Vocabulary alone is insufficient.

## Use available evidence

Prefer user-authored material in the target genre, followed by explicit preferences and corrections, then conversational messages. Use assistant drafts as positive examples only if the user clearly accepted their voice. Separate quoted material from the user's own prose. Dictation artifacts, typos, or a moment of irritation should not become signature features.

When chat retrieval is available and the user requests it, inspect a bounded set of relevant threads. First review titles or metadata, then selected user turns. State the actual scope in the private work record. Do not claim access to a complete library or invent samples from memory. Do not scan broad personal directories to find more writing.

## Notice decisions, not just word counts

Consider how the author opens a thought, builds an inference, changes direction, uses concrete detail, addresses disagreement, and ends. Notice where emphasis accumulates and where the author leaves room for the reader. Distinguish an intentional stance from an accidental grammatical problem.

Record explicit preferences as confirmed. Mark inferred patterns as provisional and attach their context. A writer may use expansive narrative in essays and concise direct language in correspondence.

For substantive work, identify positive evidence as well as dislikes: which details the author selects, how ideas are connected, where emphasis accumulates, how much is left implicit, and what kind of ending the author uses. Preserve strong original passages and record why they work. If finished prose is absent, build only from supported preferences and the current brief; do not invent an exemplar or apply an inferred conversational trait to every genre.

With sparse samples, start with the clearest supported choice in those samples and preserve it where the task fits. Leave other choices tentative. Do not assign universal weights to endings, openings, or syntax; their importance depends on what the actual author material shows.

If there is enough comparable prose, sentence-length distribution, paragraph sizes, punctuation, contractions, repeated syntax, and opening or closing patterns can help reveal drift between a draft and its source. Use them as diagnostic observations. There is no universal human sentence-length target or stylistic quota. Tiny conversational samples do not support a statistical author fingerprint.

## Keep the profile useful and private

A compact profile can contain confirmed preferences, provisional observations by genre, things the assistant has repeatedly gotten wrong, and the scope of the evidence. Include short examples only when needed and authorized. Keep it outside the shared skill repository. Never publish raw chat history or manuscript passages as part of skill distribution without separate authorization.

When feedback arrives, update the narrow rule that caused the failure. Preserve other preferences and distinguish the user's new instruction from the assistant's interpretation. An explicit present request takes priority over the profile.
