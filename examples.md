# Writing trials

These are original synthetic examples for inspecting the skill's editorial decisions. They are not private chat excerpts or quotations from Buhner. They illustrate a first manual application; author feedback is still needed to judge personal voice.

## Argument

Brief: Explain that a two-day test cannot establish what happens in a year. Be direct and conversational. Preserve the possibility that the thing works.

Draft with canned structure:

> It is important to distinguish short-term observation from long-term validation. This is not about dismissing the results, but about understanding their limitations. A two-day test provides insight, offers context, and generates questions. Ultimately, more research is needed to draw meaningful conclusions.

Revised:

> You tested it for two days. How does that tell us what happens after a year? It may work, but this test doesn't answer that question.

Check: The revision preserves the possibility of success, identifies the actual missing inference, and removes the abstract opening, ornamental list, and vague conclusion. The question belongs to this argument; it is not a required opening formula.

## Bio

Brief: Write two sentences using only these facts: Mara repairs bicycles, volunteers at a community workshop on Saturdays, and likes teaching people to maintain their own bikes. Use a relaxed professional voice.

Draft with promotional abstraction:

> Mara is a passionate bicycle professional whose work sits at the intersection of craftsmanship, community, and empowerment. Through her Saturday volunteering, she fosters a culture of independence and inspires others to unlock their potential.

Revised:

> Mara repairs bicycles and spends Saturdays volunteering at a community workshop. She likes teaching people how to maintain their own bikes.

Check: The revision uses every supplied fact without inventing a life story, personality assessment, or symbolic meaning. Its plainness is appropriate to the brief; a longer bio would need more actual material.

## Nonfiction observation

Brief: Preserve these facts: a gardener returns to the same bed each morning, notes which plants wilt first, and moves the watering can closer to those plants. Do not add memories or an uplifting lesson.

Draft with an imposed moral:

> Each morning, the gardener returns to the bed with curiosity and care. By observing, adapting, and responding, the gardener discovers that tending plants is not merely a task—it is a lesson in attentiveness. In the end, the garden teaches us to listen.

Revised:

> Each morning the gardener returns to the same bed and notes which plants wilt first. The watering can moves closer to those plants.

Check: The action carries the attention. The revision stops at the supported detail and avoids turning it into a universal lesson. It makes no claim about the gardener's feelings.
